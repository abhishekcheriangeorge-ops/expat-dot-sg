If you get hurt at work here, two pieces of folklore will reach you before the facts do. One says an EP holder isn't covered by WICA at all. The other says that if your employer never bought work injury insurance, there's nothing to claim. Both are wrong, and they're wrong for the same reason: **being covered by the Act and being covered by a policy are two different things**. WICA covers eligible employees, EP holders included. The duty to buy work injury compensation insurance is a much narrower one that only bites for certain workers. So a well-paid non-manual EP holder whose employer never bought a policy for that salary band can still claim. The employer simply pays it out of their own pocket.

## Covered by the Act, covered by a policy: not the same thing

Coverage under the Act follows your employment relationship, not your pay packet. If you work under a contract of service you're in, local or foreign, whatever your salary, age, or citizenship, and MOM's FAQ says so about EP holders in as many words. The people left outside are independent contractors and the self-employed, domestic workers, and the uniformed services.

The insurance duty is drawn far more tightly. An employer must buy work injury compensation insurance for all manual workers whatever they earn, and for non-manual employees earning S$2,600 a month or less. That definition leaves most EP holders out by design. So when HR tells you there's no policy covering someone on your salary, they may well be telling the truth, and it changes nothing about whether you can claim. It only changes who writes the cheque.

Don't let any of this get tangled up with Foreign Worker Medical Insurance, which sets inpatient floors for Work Permit and S Pass holders, or with your company's private group hospital plan. All three can end up touching the same bill. None of them is a substitute for a work-injury claim.

## What counts as a work injury

MOM's eligible-claims page turns on accidents arising out of and in the course of employment, which is broader than most people assume. It reaches travel during work for work purposes, so a meeting venue or a client site counts as long as you haven't gone off on a personal detour. It reaches company transport between home and the workplace, though not your ordinary commute on public transport. It reaches overseas assignments while you're employed by a local employer, and approved WFH or flexible-work duties where the work itself is what injured you. It also reaches certain occupational diseases and exposure-related conditions.

What it doesn't automatically cover is the ordinary MRT ride in, and it doesn't cover leisure travel overseas.

When HR waves you off with "it was after hours" or "you weren't in the office, so there's no claim", ask them which of the eligible-claims scenarios they think rules you out. That question alone tends to change the tone. If they stall, escalate.

## What to do on the day it happens

1. See a Singapore-registered doctor, and say out loud that this is a workplace injury so it goes in the notes. WICA medical expenses generally need Singapore doctor or dentist bills, which is why "I got it looked at in JB first" kills so many otherwise good claims.
2. Tell your employer in writing the same day. HR, your manager, the safety officer, whoever your company nominates. Include the time, the place, who saw it, and photos.
3. Keep your own copies of every MC, light-duty note, receipt and WhatsApp thread. Hand originals or digital MCs to HR as they ask for them, but never hand over the only copy.
4. Ask HR to confirm the iReport or insurer notification, in writing. For accidents from 1 Sep 2020 the claim is generally deemed made once the employer has notice, but nothing actually starts moving until MOM or the insurer has been told.
5. Don't resign mid-claim to "start fresh" without reading your pass clocks first. Cancelling a pass doesn't erase your eligibility, but it makes cashflow and hospital letters of guarantee a great deal uglier. [Between jobs and STVP](/guides/between-jobs-stvp-singapore) walks through that.

## What you can actually claim, and where it stops

| Benefit | Orientation (verify live) |
| --- | --- |
| Medical leave wages | Working days on MC, light duty or hospitalisation leave, up to 1 year from the accident |
| Medical expenses | Treatment within 1 year, capped at S$53,000 for accidents from 1 Nov 2025 (it was S$45,000 before) |
| Permanent incapacity / death | Lump-sum tables with a minimum and a maximum; the limits rose on 1 Nov 2025 |

The eligible medical lines are wider than people expect: consultation, ward charges, treatment, medication, ambulance, dental where it's prescribed, and some rehab and TCM when a Singapore-registered practitioner prescribes it. Where it stops is private hospital upgrades and anything that isn't work-related. Those are insurance and letter-of-guarantee problems rather than WICA ones, so read this next to [expat health insurance](/guides/health-insurance-expat) and [hospital deposits and LOGs](/guides/healthcare-gp-hospital).

One fork you can't walk back: **it's WICA or a common law claim, not both**. Withdrawal forms exist if you start down the WICA route and then decide to sue instead, but that's a decision to make with advice, not on a bad afternoon.

## When HR goes quiet

Start by getting written confirmation that MOM or the designated insurer was actually notified, because "we're handling it" and "we've filed it" are not the same sentence. If medical leave wages or bills are sitting unpaid, go to the designated insurer first and then to MOM. Disputes that aren't really about the injury, like an unpaid salary or a badly computed final payment, belong at [TADM](/guides/salary-dispute-tadm-mom) instead. And if anyone starts hinting at pass cancellation while you're still in a hospital bed, that's a separate immigration and employment mess sitting on top of the claim; keep your embassy's number and the [emergency numbers list](/guides/emergencies-995-999-embassies) somewhere you can reach one-handed.

Most routine claims settle without a lawyer. Permanent incapacity assessments and arguments about what caused what are where counsel earns the fee.
