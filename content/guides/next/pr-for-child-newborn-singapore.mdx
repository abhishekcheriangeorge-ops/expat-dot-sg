The post-birth threads on r/askSingapore tangle up three completely separate legal events: registering the birth in Singapore, sorting out the baby's foreign citizenship, and Permanent Residence. They're not the same thing and they don't happen automatically in sequence. Your blue IC does not mint PR for your child. What ICA publishes is a sponsored PR application, for an unmarried child aged below 21, born within a legal marriage to a Singapore citizen or PR, or legally adopted by one.

## What ICA actually publishes

| Eligibility ICA publishes | Who logs in |
| --- | --- |
| Unmarried child under 21, born in a legal marriage to an SC/PR parent, or legally adopted by an SC/PR | The parent sponsors via ICA e-Service with Singpass |
| Spouse of SC/PR | Spouse sponsors (different form path) |
| Aged parent of an SC aged 21+ | Child sponsors (SC-only parent path, not open to PR sponsors) |

This guide is only the child row. Sponsoring a spouse is a different form and a different set of expectations, covered in [PR for spouse](/guides/pr-for-spouse-singapore), and bringing aged parents over is [PR vs LTVP](/guides/aged-parents-pr-ltvp-singapore). If you're an EP holder thinking about your own PR through PTS or otherwise, that stays on [PR application strategy](/guides/pr-application-strategy).

## Being born here doesn't make a child PR

Having Singapore printed as the place of birth on a birth certificate is, by itself, worth nothing in immigration terms. **It creates neither PR nor citizenship for a foreign child.** Citizenship follows bloodline and registration rules set out on ICA's citizenship pages. PR follows a discretionary application that someone has to actually file and that ICA is free to refuse. So when a forum reply says "baby was born here, so automatic PR", it's wrong twice over: wrong on PR, and usually wrong on citizenship too unless one parent's own citizenship path reaches the child.

What the first week or two actually looks like for most PR parents is more mundane. You register the birth through LifeSG or the ICA birth process, as eligible. You secure the child's foreign passport, with consular registration if that's what your country requires. Then you make the real decision, which is whether to sponsor PR now, wait a while, or aim at citizenship later if an SC parent path exists. That third one is a genuine fork, and it deserves more than a fortnight of sleep-deprived thinking.

## Fees ICA publishes (verify live)

| Stage | Published fee |
| --- | --- |
| At submission | S$100 per application |
| At completion of formalities | S$20 Entry Permit + S$50 for a 5-year Re-Entry Permit + S$50 Singapore Identity Card (when IC is issued) |

ICA cites around 6 months for processing when the documents are in order, and some files simply take longer than that. Outcomes come by email and through MyICA. Every applicant added to the form has to review the declarations personally, using Singpass or ICA-issued credentials, which is a small thing that stalls a surprising number of submissions.

## NS is not a footnote for sons

This is the part to slow down on. ICA's PR page states plainly that male applicants granted PR **under their parents' sponsorship**, and foreign students granted PR, are liable for National Service. They register at around 16½ and are scheduled for full-time NS at the earliest opportunity from 18, with the ordinary pre-tertiary deferment limits. **University is not a deferment.** That last point is where a lot of families discover, far too late, that the plan they'd built around him heading straight off to an overseas degree doesn't work.

The Reddit refrain of "don't apply for PR for a baby boy" is risk framing rather than ICA policy, and it's too blunt to be useful. But if you are going to sponsor a son, do it with your eyes open. Read [CMPB](https://www.cmpb.gov.sg/) and our [citizenship and NS implications](/guides/citizenship-ns-implications) guide before you start treating PR as a school-fee optimisation, because that is how the decision usually gets framed and it is the wrong frame. And understand what happens at the other end: renouncing later without completing NS can damage his own and the wider family's future long-term immigration standing. [Renouncing PR](/guides/renouncing-pr-singapore) and the [NS Exit Permit](/guides/ns-exit-permit-pr-families) guides cover how that plays out.

## What actually drives the decision

ICA assesses family ties, economic contributions, qualifications, age, family profile and length of residency. A parent who already holds PR genuinely helps the family-ties picture, and it is still not a guarantee of anything. Newborns get rejected. So do teenagers. If it happens, apply the same hygiene we suggest in [PR rejection and reapply](/guides/pr-rejection-reapply) rather than filing again every quarter and hoping the queue eventually blinks.

On documents: certified true copies, with official English translations where they're needed. ICA lists the embassy and notary translation patterns it accepts on the PR page. It does not endorse commercial "PR agents", which is worth remembering when one quotes you for a success-based fee on something they cannot influence.

## After the IPA, it's still adult-style formalities

An approval isn't the end of it. The child still has formalities to complete: an Entry Permit, the same Re-Entry Permit discipline every PR lives with when travelling, and IC issuance once they're old enough. Put the child's REP into the household calendar alongside everyone else's, using [after getting PR](/guides/after-getting-pr-checklist) and [REP while overseas](/guides/re-entry-permit-pr-overseas). Do not assume your own REP covers your child's travel status, because it doesn't, and the moment you find out is at an immigration counter.

## When to file

Sponsor a child's PR application when you mean roots, and when you're being honest with yourself about NS for a son. Not because the birth certificate happens to say Singapore. Register the birth, secure the foreign papers, read CMPB properly if the child is male, then file the ICA child path with the fees and about six months of patience budgeted.
