Singapore isn't a monsoon-flood city in the 1970s sense any more. PUB's flood-prone area has shrunk from thousands of hectares down to a small published hotspot list, and it matters. What hasn't gone away is the flash flood: the storm that strands cars in an underpass, fills a condo basement, and turns a ground-floor lobby into an ankle-deep pond for an hour. If you viewed the place on a sunny Sunday, you saw none of it.

## What PUB actually says

Flash floods are usually short and very local, often under an hour, and they take exceptionally heavy rain to set off, not the ordinary afternoon downpour. Separately, coastal flooding can reach low-lying belts when a high tide coincides with a storm; PUB cites places like East Coast Park. And there's a live hotspot list that gets updated, which means an agent telling you "it never floods here" is making a marketing claim, and you can check it yourself.

## Pre-LOI checks that matter more than the pool

| What to check | Why it comes up on forums |
| --- | --- |
| The PUB hotspot list for the street and underpass you'll actually use | Marketing photos never show the dip in the road |
| Basement versus surface parking | A flooded basement means a dead season-parking bay and several weeks of Grab |
| Ground-floor and walk-up electrical risers | Water and DB boards together is an outage story, not a puddle story |
| Whether the drain grating outside the block is choked with leaf litter | Localised ponding is usually maintenance as much as rainfall |
| How close you are to low-lying coastal edges | High tide plus a storm hits park connectors and some estate boundaries |

If you can, walk the approach road once after a heavy afternoon storm. And ask the guardhouse directly: when did the basement last take water? They remember, even when the agent has forgotten.

## Neighbourhood realism, not a ban list

The East Coast and Katong belts win on lifestyle, and nothing here says don't live there. It just means modelling what a coastal-plus-heavy-rain day does to your park runs and your low approach road. In the older central pockets near canals and low roads, check hotspots street by street rather than by MRT station name, because the station tells you nothing about the last hundred metres. In the growth towns, active construction temporarily changes how water drains, so pair this with a [site-noise walk](/guides/construction-site-noise-renters) while you're there anyway.

Our entity pages for [East Coast](/neighbourhoods/east-coast), [Katong and Joo Chiat](/neighbourhoods/katong-joo-chiat), and [Queenstown](/neighbourhoods/queenstown) are shortlisting tools, not flood certificates. Re-check PUB regardless.

## When the water is already in the lobby

1. Move cars out of the basement, if it's safe and the MA or security are telling you to.
2. Photograph the water lines, the damaged goods, and the meter and DB areas. That's your paper trail for both the landlord and the insurer.
3. Report blocked drains and estate problems through OneService, the Town Council, or the MA. Venting in the condo WhatsApp group is not a report and nobody is actioning it.
4. **Don't treat a one-hour flash flood as an automatic lease break.** Use the repair covenants in your TA and negotiate from there. If the unit is genuinely uninhabitable for longer, that's a different conversation: [early termination](/guides/early-lease-termination-tenants).
