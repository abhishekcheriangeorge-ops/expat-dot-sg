Relocation stress, trailing-partner isolation, academic pressure on the kids, a work culture that rewards never switching off: all of it is common here, and more discussable than new arrivals expect. What actually catches families out isn't stigma. It's the invoice. A lot of employer outpatient plans **exclude psychology and counselling entirely**, and you tend to discover that in the week you need care rather than the week you sign up ([one non-resident's search for options](https://www.reddit.com/r/askSingapore/comments/1byriih/seeking_options_for_mental_health_help_as_a/), [parents working out the ADHD assessment route](https://www.reddit.com/r/askSingapore/comments/1hg0srx/what_is_the_general_process_for_seeking_advice_on/)). If your child is already medicated, what you need is continuity with a local psychiatrist rather than a foreign prescription you keep refilling, and we cover that separately in [kids and ADHD medication](/guides/kids-adhd-medication-singapore).

## Crisis first

If you or someone else is in immediate danger, call emergency services or go straight to a hospital emergency department. For suicidal crisis, Samaritans of Singapore runs **1767**, around the clock. IMH provides 24-hour emergency psychiatric assessment.

For everything short of crisis, there's the national mindline 1771, also 24/7, reachable by phone, on WhatsApp at 6669 1771, or by webchat through mindline.sg. Youth aged 16–30 living in Singapore can use [CHAT](https://www.nhghealth.com.sg/imh/chat/get-help) for a confidential assessment instead, which is often an easier first door than a GP. If the problem is showing up at school, read our parent playbook on what an international school counsellor can and can't do, where confidentiality stops, and how to escalate: [teen mental health and school counselling](/guides/teen-mental-health-school-counselling-singapore).

Keep your embassy's consular welfare contacts written down somewhere findable as well. They're a parallel line for nationals abroad, and the moment you want them is not the moment you want to be searching for them.

## What your insurance actually covers

The pattern is consistent enough to be worth checking before anything goes wrong. Get it in writing, not over the phone.

| Source | What to verify in writing | Common expat surprise |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Employer group plan | Psychiatry, psychology, counselling line items; annual caps; session limits | "Outpatient covered" still excludes mental health |
| Dependent rider | Whether DP spouses and kids inherit the same outpatient mental-health benefits | Dependents often thinner than employee cover |
| International top-up | Pre-existing waiting periods; outpatient mental-health sub-limits | Expensive plans can still carve out history |
| Public / charity pathways | IMH via GP referral; SAMH / Fei Yue counselling | EP and DP holders usually pay unsubsidised rates even at public facilities |

That last row is the one people underestimate. Going public doesn't reliably make it cheaper for you the way it would for a citizen, so the private-versus-public decision is more about wait times and fit than price.

For private sessions, community price talk clusters somewhere around S$80–250+, depending on how senior the clinician is and what they practise. Treat that as budgeting context rather than a quote, and confirm the clinic's current fees before you book.

Audit your benefits schedule the same week you land, alongside everything else in [health insurance for expats](/guides/health-insurance-expat). If the plan turns out to be thin, decide then whether an international top-up is worth buying or whether you'll just self-fund a monthly session budget. Deciding this in advance is much easier than deciding it while someone in the house is struggling.

## Getting a child assessed without losing a term

If you suspect ADHD or a learning difference in an EP or DP child, start by asking the school what they're seeing and whether they'll observe in class. That costs nothing and it gives any clinician something concrete to work with. If insurance won't cover a public wait, book a private child psychiatrist or developmental paediatrician directly; foreigners often pay broadly similar unsubsidised rates at KK as at a private clinic, so the wait is usually the deciding factor rather than the fee.

Keep every report in a portable format, because you'll need them again for [SEN school admissions](/guides/special-needs-support) and possibly in the next country. Community agencies like Unlocking ADHD are genuinely useful for orienting parents and for not feeling like the only family going through this, but they aren't a substitute for clinical assessment and they don't pretend to be.

## Finding adult care

Roughly in order of how fast you can get through the door:

- Your employer's EAP is often the quickest first call. Check the confidentiality wording and how many sessions you actually get before you start a course of work you'll have to abandon halfway.
- A GP or family doctor is useful triage, and the route to a psychiatrist referral if medication is on the table.
- Private psychologists and counsellors: match the modality to the problem — CBT, trauma work, couples — rather than picking on clinic branding.
- Charity and community counselling through SAMH, Fei Yue and similar is often open to non-residents at published rates.
- Home-country teletherapy sometimes works, if licensing allows it and your insurer will pay for cross-border care. Check both.
- Belonging, in the ordinary sense. [Finding your people](/guides/find-my-people-singapore) sits alongside clinical care. It doesn't replace it, and clinical care doesn't replace it either.

## First 90 days

- Know 1771, 1767, and where the nearest hospital ED is
- Confirm EAP access and read the mental-health outpatient wording
- Identify two clinicians, or one clinic plus the EAP, that you'd actually pick up the phone to
- If you're the trailing partner, put support in place before isolation becomes the default setting — see [trailing partner careers](/guides/trailing-partner-career-singapore) and [dual-career couples](/guides/dual-career-spouse-singapore)

Waiting until somebody can't cope is a bad way to run this. Dual-career parent burnout and the session limits that come with EAP cover get their own playbook in [parental burnout and EAP](/guides/parental-burnout-eap-singapore). Early, boring, slightly unnecessary-feeling support is a lot cheaper than a crisis in the middle of a posting.
