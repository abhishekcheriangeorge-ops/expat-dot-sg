Tech and finance offers wave equity around as if it were free money bolted onto the salary. Under Singapore's rules it usually isn't a bonus at all — it's employment income, taxed when it vests or when you exercise. And leaving the country can pull a **deemed exercise** bill forward so it lands before your last payslip clears.

## When the tax actually lands

| Plan type | Usual Singapore tax point | Taxable amount (conceptually) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| RSU / share award (ESOW with vesting) | When shares vest, or when a sale restriction lifts | Open-market value at that date − price you paid (often S$0) |
| Stock options (ESOP) | When you exercise | Market value at exercise − exercise price |
| Later share-price rise after the tax point | Generally not a Singapore capital-gains tax event | Banking / FX costs still matter |

The thing to hold onto is that the tax point follows your employment, not your address on the day. If you were exercising your employment in Singapore when the grant was made, it stays Singapore-taxable even if the parent is US-listed and you're sitting overseas when it vests. Grants tied purely to overseas employment are a different analysis, and that's a question for HR or a tax adviser rather than something to assume your way out of.

## When your broker and your payroll never speak to each other

Singapore employers are supposed to report ESOP and ESOW gains on IR8A, with Appendix 8B. What goes wrong is structural rather than malicious: a US parent runs the equity plan, local payroll is outsourced to a third party, and the vest files never make the journey between them. Payroll doesn't know the vest happened, so it doesn't report it, and you find out when you're staring at a return with a large number missing from it.

| Situation | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| Local entity + payroll | Ask HR for the Appendix 8B draft when a large vest hits |
| EOR / no local entity | You may still have Singapore employment income. Call IRAS or a tax agent, and don't leave the gains off simply because payroll says it can't file them |
| Sell-to-cover withholding | Check whether Singapore tax was actually remitted, or only US broker tax |

That last row is the one that lulls people. Seeing tax withheld at the broker feels like the matter has been settled, and it may only have settled the US side of it.

## The exit trap: deemed exercise

For foreign employees leaving Singapore employment — and in some PR situations too — IRAS applies a deemed exercise rule. Unexercised options and unvested awards can be taxed at tax clearance rather than years later, which means the bill can arrive while you're packing, calculated on shares you don't hold yet and can't sell. It runs alongside [IR21 withholding](/guides/tax-clearance-when-leaving), so the two tend to land together.

Three things worth doing before you resign or accept an overseas posting:

1. Ask HR whether the company uses IRAS tracking options, which defer the tax to the actual vest or exercise under approved schemes.
2. Model the cash you'd need if a large block of unvested RSUs is deemed taxable at exit.
3. Don't schedule a huge vest for the week after you cancel your EP without reading the clearance calendar first.

## Don't underwrite the rent with RSUs

Equity belongs in your [salary package decoding](/guides/salary-package-decoding) math, but discount it three times before you believe it. Start with the expected annual vest in SGD at a conservative share price. Take off Singapore tax at your expected marginal rate. Then take off again for lock-ups, blackouts, and the fact that the whole lot is one company's stock.

Whatever survives that is real money, and it still isn't rent money. Landlords and schools want a fixed number on a fixed date and won't be moved by the share price. Use your salary for those, and treat equity as savings-rate fuel instead.
