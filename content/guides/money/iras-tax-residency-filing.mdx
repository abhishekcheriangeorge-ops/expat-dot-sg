Singapore taxes individuals primarily on Singapore-sourced income, which is the easy part. The harder part is tax residency, because it decides which rate table you sit on and which reliefs you can claim, and it is **not the same thing as your immigration status**. Being on an EP doesn't make you a tax resident, and neither does holding PR settle the question on its own. Every arrival season, r/askSingapore fills up with the same idea: push the start date to July and pay less tax. The answer lives in IRAS's day-count tests rather than in folklore, and it usually isn't the answer people are hoping for. The other recurring panic arrives later. That's the first Notice of Assessment, a single bill covering a year you have already lived through and spent. GIRO instalments take the sting out of it, but only if the plan is approved before the due date.

## Why residency matters

The short version, simplified:

| Status (simplified) | What people care about |
| --- | --- |
| Tax resident | Progressive resident rates, plus reliefs you may be able to claim |
| Non-resident | Employment income is treated differently, and there are fewer reliefs |
| Leaving mid-assignment | Your employer runs IR21 tax clearance and withholds. See our leaving guides |

IRAS documents a handful of routes into residency. The straightforward one is spending 183 days or more here in a calendar year, on the presence and employment-exercise tests as IRAS defines them. Then there is the straddling route, which is what most mid-year arrivals actually end up using: continuous employment across two calendar years, with a total stay of at least 183 days. And there is the work-pass route, where IRAS treats a foreigner issued a pass of at least one year as a tax resident while employed.

That last one has a tail worth understanding on day one rather than on the way out. Residency is reviewed at IR21 clearance, so if your total stay comes in under 183 days you can be reclassified as a non-resident when you leave, after a year of being treated as though you weren't.

Run the official tests against your own dates rather than assembling a hybrid rule out of forum posts. And if a foreign broker or tax office wants proof that you are a Singapore tax resident so it can apply treaty relief, that is a separate Certificate of Residence application, which we cover in [COR and DTA](/guides/certificate-of-residence-dta).

## What your employer files, and what stays yours

Most employers transmit employment income to IRAS automatically. In the simple case, your job is to check the pre-filled figures, declare anything else that's taxable, and pay the Notice of Assessment when it arrives. "Simple case" is doing a lot of work in that sentence, though. These are still yours to get right:

- Benefits in kind. Housing, school fees and the rest of the package all need to be characterised correctly.
- Side income or consulting, which is really three questions wearing one coat: does your pass allow it, what does the contract say, and how is it taxed.
- Equity and RSUs granted by a foreign parent.
- Dual-country filing, if your home country taxes worldwide income. US persons, most obviously.

## Cashflow timing for year one

Singapore personal income tax is largely paid in arrears, and that single fact is behind most first-year cash crunches. Start in September and you can receive a material bill the following year covering months you have already lived and already budgeted around. Nothing came out of your monthly salary along the way to cover it.

What works in practice is unglamorous. Keep a tax sinking fund from month one, using a rough rate from your own package modelling or, better, from your adviser. Once the assessment lands, look at whether GIRO instalments are available for your case. And don't let "no monthly withholding" quietly become "no tax" in your head. It's the same tax, just presented as one number much later.

### When the NOA lands: pay it, or get on GIRO

IRAS's guide for new taxpayers is blunt about the deadline. Tax is generally payable within 1 month of the NOA date. If you can't pay in full, you can apply for up to 12 interest-free monthly GIRO instalments through myTax Portal, under Account → Apply/Manage GIRO Plan. These are the clocks that catch people:

| Rule | Why foreigners miss it |
| --- | --- |
| GIRO must be **approved before** the NOA due date | Setting it up late still triggers the 5% late-payment penalty |
| The new online eGIRO often takes around 3 working days | Hardcopy GIRO can take weeks, so don't mail a form on day 28 |
| Deductions commonly come out on the 6th | The cash has to be in the salary account before deduction day |
| Longer plans need IRAS to approve them | Use the difficulty or longer-plan path if 12 months isn't enough |

Pay even if you think the figures are wrong. Amend through the published services by all means, but an objection is not a payment holiday and IRAS doesn't treat it as one. Leaving Singapore part-way through a year is a separate problem with its own timetable: [IR21 clearance](/guides/tax-clearance-when-leaving).

## Myths worth dropping

- "EP means tax resident forever." Related, not identical. The ≥1-year pass treatment can reverse at clearance if your stay is &lt;183 days.
- "Money sitting overseas is invisible forever." Where the income is sourced and where you did the work matter far more than whether you moved the cash.
- "Timing entry to 1 July always halves your tax." The straddling rules and your home country's exit tax usually dominate the arithmetic.
- "Flat 15% on the whole package in year one." Only if you are actually assessed as a non-resident. Plenty of mid-year arrivals end up on straddling or ≥1-year pass treatment instead, so model both before you plan around either.

If your package includes tax equalisation, get HR or the vendor to spell out who files what before you arrive, not in April when everyone is busy. Next: [foreign income](/guides/foreign-income-and-tax) and [tax clearance when leaving](/guides/tax-clearance-when-leaving).
