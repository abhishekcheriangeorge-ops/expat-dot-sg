The clinical quality here is genuinely high. Uninsured private care is also genuinely expensive, and those two facts arrive together. As a foreign work-pass holder you sit outside the Medisave–MediShield architecture that citizens and PRs are built into, so the medical plan in your offer letter isn't a perk. It's real compensation, or it's a hole you will be funding yourself. That flips once you get PR, when MediShield Life auto-enrols you and you're suddenly inside the resident stack; the day-one admin is in [after getting PR](/guides/after-getting-pr-checklist). One thing worth filing away early: an injury at work runs on the WICA track first, not only as a group-hospital claim, and people lose money by treating the two as the same thing ([WICA for EP and S Pass holders](/guides/wica-work-injury-compensation-ep)).

## First principle: you are not on the local subsidy stack

CPF contributions, and the whole savings-and-insurance structure built on top of them, apply to Singapore Citizens and PRs. Ordinary EP and S Pass holders aren't in that system at all, so the instinct to assume you can just use public subsidies like everyone around you doesn't survive contact with the bill. At a public hospital you're typically billed as a **private, unsubsidised** patient unless a specific scheme says otherwise. The clinical care is the same. The price is not.

This also explains one of the more common mis-purchases. EP holders shopping for what a colleague described as an "Integrated Shield" plan are almost always buying a cash-pay foreigner hospital plan. It may be a perfectly good product. It simply isn't the MediSave-funded thing your citizen colleague is comparing it to, so make sure you're comparing like with like before you decide one is overpriced.

## Audit your employer plan in the first week

Ask HR in writing for the benefits schedule. Not a one-line "medical is covered" email, but the actual schedule with the numbers in it. Then read it against the list below, because every row here is somebody's expensive surprise.

| Question | Why it bites |
| --- | --- |
| Inpatient annual / lifetime caps | A low cap survives a broken wrist and fails on ICU or major surgery |
| Outpatient GP / specialist limits | Plenty of "plans" are inpatient-heavy and thin where you actually use them |
| Panel vs non-panel reimbursement | Going off-panel can mean a 20–40%+ co-pay |
| Dependants included? At what cost? | Trailing spouses are routinely under-covered |
| Maternity waiting period / exclusion | Total exclusion is common, not exotic |
| Pre-existing condition treatment | Underwriting or a group waiver — these are very different answers |
| Mental health / physio / dental / optical | Frequently carved out entirely |
| Evacuation / cover outside Singapore | Critical the moment you start travelling regionally |
| Claims process (cashless vs reimbursement) | Decides whether being ill also means being out of pocket |
| Letter of Guarantee (LOG) process | Without one, hospitals ask foreigners for large deposits |

The r/askSingapore threads about top-up insurance keep turning up the same shape: a corporate plan that reads perfectly well until somebody lands in ICU. If your schedule looks like S$60k inpatient with a S$10k ICU sub-limit, treat that as a red flag rather than something to be pleased about.

For anything elective, get the LOG workflow out of HR or your broker days ahead of the date, not on the morning. The appointment side of that is in the [GP and hospital guide](/guides/healthcare-gp-hospital).

### The Letter of Guarantee, and the cashflow trap behind it

Private hospitals routinely ask foreigners for a large deposit unless an insurer has issued a Letter of Guarantee **before** admission. The LOG is just the hospital being told in writing who is paying. Without one you can be fully insured and still be asked for cash at the counter, which is an unpleasant thing to discover while you're being admitted.

| Moment | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| Elective surgery booked | Ask HR or the broker for the LOG days ahead; cashless billing fails without it |
| Emergency admission | Get someone to call the broker as soon as they can; until then, expect a deposit |
| LOG wording vs B1 or private ward | Pro-ration and co-insurance still apply; a LOG is not a blank cheque for a private suite |
| No MediSave as an EP holder | Ignore the peer advice about draining MediSave first — that path belongs to citizens and PRs |

One thing the table can't say loudly enough: a LOG only works while the policy is live. If your group cover has already ended, so has the guarantee.

## When families buy private or international top-ups

Three patterns cover most of what families here actually do. A top-up on its own fills the outpatient gap or lifts the inpatient ceiling above what the group plan allows, and it's the cheap fix when the employer plan is broadly sound. A full international plan, meaning Allianz, Cigna and similar regional products, is what you reach for when it isn't; peer quotes for a young family tend to land in the high four to mid five figures SGD per year, and the two things moving that number are maternity and outpatient cover.

The third pattern is a maternity rider, and it has to be timed before conception. Once your spouse is pregnant, new cover for that pregnancy is usually impossible to buy at any price, which is why this is the one insurance decision that genuinely cannot wait. If you're already pregnant, plan on cash-pay delivery at a private hospital plus whatever the group plan still covers, and read [maternity care](/guides/maternity-care-singapore) for what that costs.

## Job change, notice period, and cover that doesn't travel

Group medical is an employment benefit, not a personal policy you carry to the next company. However long you've had it, it was never yours.

| Moment | What usually happens (confirm with HR and the schedule) |
| --- | --- |
| Serving notice | Cover typically runs to your **last employment day**, not to the day you mentally checked out |
| Last day / EP cancel | Group cover ends, and dependants on the same schedule drop with you |
| New employer | New underwriting, or a new group waiver; waiting periods may reset from zero |
| Allowance instead of insurance | Cash can fund a personal plan that survives job hops, though the S$300/mo figure people quote rarely stretches to full family inpatient |

The resignation-notice and "medical allowance vs insurance" threads keep rediscovering the same point: corporate cover is excellent right up until it isn't. Price a personal top-up before you need elective surgery in the middle of a job hop, particularly if you're changing employers and will face [IR21 withholding](/guides/tax-clearance-when-leaving) on your final pay. The month you're least covered can easily be the same month your pay is being held.

## Public vs private care as a foreigner

Most expats end up using all three settings at some point, and they behave quite differently.

| Setting | What expats usually experience |
| --- | --- |
| Private GP / specialist | Fast, English-heavy clinics, and panel rules that decide what you get back |
| Private hospital | Predictable packages, with expensive outliers like NICU or complications |
| Public hospital as a foreigner | Excellent clinical care, billed unsubsidised — good medicine, not cheap insurance |

## A sensible buying sequence

1. Get the employer's benefits schedule PDF out of HR, in writing.
2. Map your household's actual risks: pregnancy plans, chronic medication, children playing contact sport, how much you travel.
3. Price one reputable international quote and one local top-up. Not eight. More quotes past that point buy confusion, not clarity.
4. Decide what deductible you can genuinely float for 30 days, because reimbursement plans make you the bank in the meantime.
5. Re-check the whole thing at EP renewal or when you change jobs, since coverage often quietly resets.
6. If you're leaving employment, ask HR in writing for the cover end date and the cut-off for open claims, then work through the [exit and between-jobs medical bridge](/guides/health-insurance-exit-between-jobs-bridge-singapore) before cancel day.

## Be honest on the disclosure form

Insurers investigate claims. Leaving a prior condition off the form to get a cheaper quote is how people find out their policy was paper all along, and they find out at the worst possible moment. The medical underwriting threads on the Singapore Expats Forum are blunt about this, and they're blunt for a reason.

Health insurance isn't a nice-to-have line filed under "settling in". For foreigners with no CPF medical rails underneath them, it's the thing that keeps a normal year normal.
